# Evidence memo: Silicon Sample Benchmark

**Cutoff and scope.** Evidence was retrieved through 2026-08-30. This memo contains no benchmark outcomes, pilot results, other teams' predictions, or target forecasts. The row-level extraction is in `EVIDENCE_LIBRARY.csv`; the complete 208-pair routing table is in `TARGET_EVIDENCE_MAP.csv`. Numerical claims below are published estimates unless labeled as a calculation or design implication.

## Executive synthesis

The defensible prior is **small, heterogeneous, and outcome-distance dependent**. A U.S. climate-attitude meta-analysis of 76 experiments (N=76,033; 396 effects) estimates Hedges' *g*=0.08, 95% CI [0.05, 0.10], with a prediction interval spanning −0.04 to 0.19. Policy effects were smaller than belief effects. The 63-country climate megastudy (N=59,440) found that the best interventions changed belief by 2.3% and policy support by 2.6%, versus 12.1% for information sharing; tree planting did not increase and several interventions reduced it. These results argue against propagating a proximal attitude effect mechanically into policy, donation, signup, or behavior.

The closest completed U.S. megastudy is unusually valuable: Voelkel et al. (2026; analytic N=13,544) used the same three filler controls—neckties, baseball, and dances—and randomized ten climate messages. Reported attitude effects were generally about 1–4 percentage points; donation effects were null and partisan heterogeneity was limited. Goldwert et al. (2026; N=31,324) supplies a second U.S. behavioral anchor: control rates were 22% newsletter signup, 49% donation, 41% representative letter, 19% petition, and 16% video sharing. Its strongest efficacy/emotion message increased public advocacy 10 points [8,12] and political advocacy 6 points [4,8], but those are purpose-built advocacy outcomes and should not be treated as generic climate-attitude effects.

Direct topic matches support relative distinctions, not large magnitudes. A 27-country consensus experiment (N=10,527) moved perceived consensus strongly (*d*=0.47) but produced only *d*=0.06 for climate-reality belief, 0.10 for human causation, 0.05 for worry, and a null 0.02 for public action. A U.S./U.K./India extreme-weather attribution experiment (N=9,000) found message effects of roughly *d*=0.06–0.14 on causal attribution; U.S. flood percentage wording reached *d*=0.18, while worry was at most 0.06, government action was 0.03 pooled and not significant in the U.S., and link clicking was 3.2%. A U.S. scientific-institutions experiment (N=2,545) found about two-point effects on climate belief or perceived model skill, with some intervals crossing zero. Historical analysis of 17 climate-model projections found 10 consistent with observed warming as published and 14 after correcting for forcing; this supports factual plausibility for the model-accuracy treatment but is not itself an ATE.

## What silicon-sample evidence permits

The strongest broad result is encouraging but cautions against raw use. Ashokkumar et al. (2026) evaluated 469 effects from 70 randomized studies (119,330 participants): simulated and human ATEs correlated *r*=.85 [0.81,.88], noise-adjusted .92, but calibration slope was .56 and RMSE 11.09 percentage points. In 15 large coordinated experiments (606 effects; 4.86 million participants), performance fell to *r*=.39 (adjusted .48), slope .34; survey and text experiments were about .43 and .45. A publication-cutoff subset did well (*r*=.90, slope .60), but this is not proof that a new climate benchmark will transfer.

Two complementary studies explain the risk. Li and Ji (2026) found that statistically realistic synthetic distributions were a weak proxy for causal accuracy: across international survey experiments, LLM response MAE was 24.3–26.1 points and ATE error 8.7–9.4 points, versus 4.3–4.4 for supervised prediction; behavioral outcomes were worse. Cummins et al. varied 252 defensible analysis configurations and obtained participant-rank correlations from −.24 to .40 in one dataset and −.29 to .22 in another; a cross-scale estimate ranged from −.40 to .78 against a human value of .26. Bisbee et al. found compressed variance in synthetic ANES responses, significant disagreement in 48% of regression coefficients, and sign reversals among 32% of disagreements. Therefore realistic marginals, a persuasive rationale, or a favorable single prompt cannot substitute for held-out causal validation.

Design consequence: Tier 1 should be treated as a causal simulator with known underdispersion and overstatement risk, not as synthetic microdata that become accurate merely through sample size. Tier 2 should estimate human means directly and strongly regularize unsupported subgroup interactions. Tier 3 should begin from human experiments and forecast an ATE, not infer it from a mechanism score. Independent generations must be retained regardless of whether they look surprising.

## Empirical priors by outcome family

| Family | Portable prior | Adjustment rules |
|---|---|---|
| Scientist trust, distrust, credibility, funding perceptions | Small effects are plausible when the message directly supplies process, integrity, competence, benevolence, or funding evidence. Current U.S. anchors include 65% categorical trust in climate scientists, but wording is not the benchmark's 0–100 item. | Respect headroom; a factual treatment may move a specific trust dimension without equally moving all twelve items. Distrust is not assumed to be an exact reverse of trust. |
| Climate belief and concern | Typical one-shot shifts are low single-digit percentage points. Consensus primarily corrects perceived consensus; downstream belief/worry changes are much smaller. | Allow null/negative effects. Larger updates are more plausible among initially misinformed or less familiar respondents, but population interactions require direct evidence. |
| General and specific policy | Usually more distal and smaller than belief; some tailored advocacy interventions can reach mid-single digits. | Do not infer policy effects from factual accuracy alone. Ideology and prior climate views are more defensible moderators than basic demographics. |
| Behavioral intention | Weak attitude–behavior transport. Information or norm messages can change selected intentions, including effects around .07–.09 SD in a large U.S. experiment, but generalization is narrow. | Use behavior-specific references; do not inherit an attitude ATE. |
| Donation and newsletter signup | Near-null is the default without direct behavioral evidence. Voelkel found no donation gains; Goldwert provides level and conversion benchmarks for a different advocacy task. | Forecast on original dollar/probability scales. Preserve zero and negative possibilities; treat control baselines as decisive for headroom. |

## Condition-level evidence map

Evidence strength is **moderate**, rather than high, only where a close intervention family exists: Consensus (Geiger and the meta-analysis), Extreme weather predictions (Thomas-Walters), Model accuracy and the two Measurement & modeling treatments (Hausfather and Orchinik), and Oil industry misinformation (Amazeen). Even there, exact wording, control, outcome, and population differ.

The scientist-interview, portrait, peer-review, community-helper, high-trust, and funding messages draw mainly on institutional history, national trust baselines, and general persuasion evidence. These sources support a proximal-trust ordering but do not identify exact ATEs. Corporate reliance and oil misinformation have stronger corporate-accountability/inoculation analogues. Social justice and former-skeptic framing have broad moral-frame or conservative-messenger analogues, with limited evidence for stable subgroup superiority. The precise sources used for every condition–outcome pair are listed in the map; none of its cells contains a target effect.

## Moderation, baselines, and transportability

Subgroup claims require more evidence than population effects. Consensus effects were larger when perceived consensus was initially low, but Geiger et al. found no right-wing or low-trust backfire. The global megastudy found effects mainly among nonskeptics. The closest U.S. filler-control megastudy reported little party heterogeneity. These results support partial pooling of party and pretreatment-climate moderators, and much stronger pooling of age, race/ethnicity, gender, and education interactions toward zero. They do not justify bespoke narratives for 27 moderator levels.

Current baselines are anchors, not interchangeable measures. CCAM's April 2026 U.S. survey (N=1,068) reports 68% saying global warming is happening, 59% human-caused, 66% worried, and 65% personally important. Pew's October 2024 panel (N=9,593) found 34% saying climate policies help the economy and 34% saying they hurt it. The 2024 Annenberg monitor (N=1,555) found 65% categorical trust in climate scientists. Scale, wording, field date, ordering, and sample differences must be explicit in any baseline conversion.

## Risks and design implications

1. **Outcome leakage and memorization.** Several validation papers predate the forecast model's 2026-02-16 knowledge cutoff. Historical outcomes must be hidden from calls, but memorization cannot be ruled out; post-cutoff studies are a robustness check, not a clean substitute when full stimuli are unavailable.
2. **Variance and calibration.** Synthetic respondents often understate variance while simulated ATEs overstate magnitude. Any shrinkage must be fixed from held-out studies. More participants reduce Monte Carlo noise but not structural bias.
3. **Rank versus scale.** Pooled Pearson rewards rank/dispersion; RMSE and calibration punish exaggeration. The method should preserve evidence-backed rank differences while shrinking unsupported magnitudes, never rescale forecasts merely to improve correlation.
4. **Behavioral transport.** Donation and signup require their own priors and control baselines. No attitude-to-behavior multiplier is defensible.
5. **Nulls and negatives.** Meta-analytic prediction intervals and megastudy behavior results make both necessary possibilities.

The evidence therefore favors a direct, evidence-conditioned Tier 3 entry as the provisional primary, subject to the documented historical validation. Tier 1 remains genuinely participant-level; Tier 2 remains a separate human-cell-mean forecasting task. Their base forecasts must remain mutually hidden until frozen.
